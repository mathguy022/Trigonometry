Angles and Their Measure

Angles can be measured in degrees or in radians. This section defines both, converts between them, and then puts the measure of an angle to work on arcs and sectors.

Warm-up

Question: A full circle represents a complete rotation. How many degrees are in a full circle?

Answer: $360$ degrees.

Question: If you cut a pizza into $4$ equal slices, what fraction of the pizza is one slice?

Answer: $\frac{1}{4}$. In terms of angles, this is $\frac{1}{4}$ of $360^\circ$, which is $90^\circ$.

1Defining Angles: Degrees vs. Radians

In college mathematics we primarily use two units to measure angles.

A. Degrees ($^\circ$)

A degree is defined by dividing a circle into $360$ equal parts. This is an arbitrary system, based on ancient Babylonian astronomy.

A full turn is $360^\circ$, a half turn is $180^\circ$, and a quarter turn is $90^\circ$.

B. Radians (rad)

A radian is a natural unit of measurement, based on the geometry of the circle itself.

A full turn is $2\pi$ radians — about $6.28$ of them.

Definition

One radian is the angle subtended at the center of a circle by an arc that is equal in length to the radius of the circle.

Why use radians?

They simplify calculus formulas later on. For example, the derivative of $\sin x$ is $\cos x$ only if $x$ is in radians. Every formula you learn for trigonometric functions is written assuming radians, so converting early saves trouble later.

The conversion factor

Since a full circle is $360^\circ$ and also $2\pi$ radians,

$$360^\circ = 2\pi \text{ radians}.$$

Dividing both sides by $2$, we get the fundamental relationship

$$180^\circ = \pi \text{ radians}.$$

To convert, we use the ratio:

ConversionMultiply byReason
Degrees → Radians $\dfrac{\pi}{180}$ There are $\pi$ radians in $180^\circ$.
Radians → Degrees $\dfrac{180}{\pi}$ There are $180^\circ$ in $\pi$ radians.

3Converting Measures

Let's practice converting. Remember: to go from degrees to radians, multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180}$; to go from radians to degrees, multiply by $\frac{180}{\pi}$.

Example 1

Convert $135^\circ$ to radians.

  1. Set up the multiplication. $$135\cdot\frac{\pi}{180}$$
  2. Simplify the fraction. Both numbers are divisible by $45$: $$135\div45=3 \qquad 180\div45=4$$
  3. Write the answer. $$135^\circ=\frac{3\pi}{4}\text{ radians}$$
Example 2

Convert $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ radians to degrees.

  1. Multiply by the conversion factor. $$\frac{5\pi}{6}\cdot\frac{180}{\pi}$$
  2. Cancel $\pi$ and divide $180$ by $6$. $$5\cdot30=150$$
  3. Write the answer. $$\frac{5\pi}{6}\text{ radians}=150^\circ$$

4Standard Position & Coterminal Angles

Standard position

An angle is in standard position if:

  1. Its vertex is at the origin $(0,0)$.
  2. Its initial side lies along the positive $x$-axis.

Coterminal angles

Coterminal angles share the same terminal side. You find them by adding or subtracting full rotations ($360^\circ$ or $2\pi$).

Formula

$$\text{Coterminal angle}=\theta+360^\circ\cdot k \qquad \text{or} \qquad \theta+2\pi\cdot k$$

where $k$ is any integer ($1$, $-1$, $2$, and so on).

Example

Find a positive and a negative coterminal angle for $100^\circ$.

  • Positive: $100^\circ+360^\circ=460^\circ$
  • Negative: $100^\circ-360^\circ=-260^\circ$

5Arc Length and Sector Area

These formulas rely on the proportion of the angle to the whole circle.

Important

The angle $\theta$ must be in radians for these standard formulas. If you are given degrees, convert first using $\theta_{\text{rad}}=\theta_{\text{deg}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{180}$.

ConceptFormulaDescription
Arc Length ($s$) $s=r\theta$ Length of the curved part of the circle.
Sector Area ($A$) $A=\dfrac{1}{2}r^2\theta$ Area of the “pie slice” region.

6Supplementary and Complementary Angles

These terms describe pairs of angles that add up to specific totals.

Complementary angles

Two angles whose sum is $90^\circ$ — together they form a right angle.

Example: $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$.

Supplementary angles

Two angles whose sum is $180^\circ$ — together they form a straight line.

Example: $110^\circ$ and $70^\circ$.

7Real-World Application

Problem

A car tire has a diameter of $27$ inches. If the car travels $1$ mile ($5280$ feet), how many revolutions does the tire make?

Solution

  1. Find the radius. $$r=\frac{27}{2}=13.5\text{ inches}$$
  2. Find the circumference — the distance travelled in one revolution. $$C=2\pi r=2\cdot\pi\cdot13.5\approx84.82\text{ inches}$$
  3. Convert the total distance to inches. $1$ mile is $5280$ feet, and $1$ foot is $12$ inches: $$5280\cdot12=63{,}360\text{ inches}$$
  4. Divide the total distance by the circumference. $$\frac{63{,}360}{84.82}\approx746.99$$
Answer

The tire makes approximately $\boxed{747\text{ revolutions}}$.

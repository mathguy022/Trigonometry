Trigonometry of Right Triangles

Every right triangle hides three ratios that depend only on its angles, never on its size. This section defines those ratios, derives their values for the special angles, and puts them to work.

1Similar Triangles

Two triangles are similar if they have the same shape — that is, if their corresponding angles are congruent. A second, equivalent test: two triangles are similar if and only if their corresponding sides are proportional.

Writing the proportionality of corresponding sides and clearing fractions gives

$$\frac{AB}{RS}=\frac{AB}{BC}\cdot\frac{BC}{ST}=\frac{AB}{BC}\cdot\frac{AB}{RS},$$

which simplifies to $\frac{AB}{BC}=\frac{RS}{ST}$, and similarly for the other pair. So in any two similar triangles, the ratios of corresponding side lengths are equivalent.

This correspondence is the engine of the whole section: if two right triangles share an acute angle, they are similar, and therefore every ratio of their sides is fixed by that angle alone.

2Trigonometric Ratios

Take a right triangle and name one of its acute angles $\theta$. The longest side, opposite the right angle, is the hypotenuse. Of the two shorter sides, the one touching $\theta$ is adjacent to $\theta$, and the one $\theta$ opens toward is opposite $\theta$.

Definition

Suppose $\theta$ is an acute angle in a right triangle whose hypotenuse has length $c$, whose side adjacent to $\theta$ has length $a$, and whose side opposite $\theta$ has length $b$. Then

$$\begin{aligned} \sin\theta &= \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{b}{c}\\[6pt] \cos\theta &= \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{a}{c}\\[6pt] \tan\theta &= \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \frac{b}{a} \end{aligned}$$

and the three remaining ratios are their reciprocals:

$$\begin{aligned} \csc\theta &= \frac{\text{hypotenuse}}{\text{opposite}} = \frac{c}{b}\\[6pt] \sec\theta &= \frac{\text{hypotenuse}}{\text{adjacent}} = \frac{c}{a}\\[6pt] \cot\theta &= \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{opposite}} = \frac{a}{b} \end{aligned}$$

It is common practice to write $\sin\theta$ rather than $\sin(\theta)$.

Two properties worth remembering

  1. Same angle, same ratios. For all right triangles with the same acute angle $\theta$, the triangles are similar, so the values of the trigonometric ratios of $\theta$ will be equal.
  2. Reciprocals. Cosecant, secant, and cotangent are reciprocals of sine, cosine, and tangent respectively. Once you know three, you know all six: $$\csc\theta=\frac{1}{\sin\theta},\qquad \sec\theta=\frac{1}{\cos\theta},\qquad \cot\theta=\frac{1}{\tan\theta}$$
Example 1

Use the triangle shown to evaluate $\sin\alpha$, $\cos\alpha$, $\tan\alpha$, $\csc\alpha$, $\sec\alpha$, and $\cot\alpha$.

From the definitions of the trigonometric ratios,

$$\begin{aligned} \sin\alpha &= \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{4}{5}\\[6pt] \cos\alpha &= \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{3}{5}\\[6pt] \tan\alpha &= \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \frac{4}{3} \end{aligned}$$

The reciprocals give the remaining three values:

$$\begin{aligned} \csc\alpha &= \frac{1}{\sin\alpha} = \frac{5}{4}\\[6pt] \sec\alpha &= \frac{1}{\cos\alpha} = \frac{5}{3}\\[6pt] \cot\alpha &= \frac{1}{\tan\alpha} = \frac{3}{4} \end{aligned}$$

Example 2

Verify that the two triangles below are similar, then evaluate the trigonometric ratio values for the angle in the second triangle that corresponds to $\alpha$. What can you deduce?

The side lengths of the second triangle are proportional to those of the first by a scale factor of $3$:

$$\frac{9}{3}=\frac{12}{4}=\frac{15}{5}=3$$

So the triangles are similar, and $\beta=\alpha$ in measure. Writing opp, adj, and hyp for brevity:

$$\begin{aligned} \sin\beta &= \frac{12}{15} = \frac{3\cdot4}{3\cdot5} = \frac{4}{5}\\[6pt] \cos\beta &= \frac{9}{15} = \frac{3\cdot3}{3\cdot5} = \frac{3}{5}\\[6pt] \tan\beta &= \frac{12}{9} = \frac{3\cdot4}{3\cdot3} = \frac{4}{3} \end{aligned}$$

Comparing with Example 1, the values are identical. We conclude that trigonometric ratios are independent of the size of the triangle — they depend only on the angle.

3The Pythagorean Theorem

The Pythagorean Theorem

In a right triangle, the square of the length of the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares of the lengths of its legs. In the triangle shown, $c^2=a^2+b^2$.

Solving for the hypotenuse gives $c=\pm\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, and since $c$ is a length it must be positive, so $c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. Likewise $a^2=c^2-b^2$ gives $a=\sqrt{c^2-b^2}$.

4Special Right Triangles

Now that we have the Pythagorean theorem, we can find exact ratio values for the angles $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$, and $60^\circ$.

Ratios of $30^\circ$–$60^\circ$–$90^\circ$ triangles

We begin with $30^\circ$. Sketch a $30^\circ$–$60^\circ$–$90^\circ$ triangle with hypotenuse $c$ and envisage it as half of an equilateral triangle.

The equilateral triangle has three $60^\circ$ angles and all sides equal to $c$.
The altitude from the apex bisects both the base and the apex angle, cutting the $60^\circ$ angle into two $30^\circ$ angles and forming a right angle at the base.
Let $x$ be the shortest side (opposite the $30^\circ$ angle). Then the hypotenuse is $c=2x$, and the Pythagorean theorem gives $a=\sqrt{(2x)^2-x^2}=\sqrt{3}\,x$.
The triangle is right isosceles, so the two legs are equal. Calling each $x$, the theorem gives $c=\sqrt{x^2+x^2}=\sqrt{2}\,x$.
So in a $45^\circ$–$45^\circ$–$90^\circ$ triangle the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{2}$ times a leg — and both acute angles have $\sin$ and $\cos$ equal to $\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$.

Step through the derivation, then use the results to find the exact ratio values below.

Ratio values for the standard angles

Using $c=2x$ and $a=\sqrt{3}\,x$ for the $30^\circ$ triangle, the definitions give

$$\sin30^\circ=\frac{1}{2},\qquad \cos30^\circ=\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\qquad \tan30^\circ=\frac{\sqrt3}{3},$$

and taking reciprocals,

$$\csc30^\circ=2,\qquad \sec30^\circ=\frac{2\sqrt3}{3},\qquad \cot30^\circ=\sqrt3.$$

You may rationalize the denominator of $\tan30^\circ$ to get $\frac{1}{\sqrt3}=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{3}$. These values apply to any $30^\circ$ angle. For the $45^\circ$–$45^\circ$–$90^\circ$ triangle, $c=\sqrt2\,x$ and both legs are $x$, so

$$\sin45^\circ=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2},\qquad \cos45^\circ=\frac{\sqrt2}{2},\qquad \tan45^\circ=1,$$ $$\csc45^\circ=\sqrt2,\qquad \sec45^\circ=\sqrt2,\qquad \cot45^\circ=1.$$

The $60^\circ$ values follow from the complements, or directly from the same triangle read from the other acute angle. Summarizing all three standard angles:

5Solving Standard Right Triangles

We use the values in the table, along with the ratios of the $30^\circ$–$60^\circ$–$90^\circ$ and $45^\circ$–$45^\circ$–$90^\circ$ triangles, to determine missing angles and sides. This is called solving right triangles.

Example 3

A right triangle has one angle of $60^\circ$ and a hypotenuse of length $20$. Find the unknown side lengths and the missing angle measure.

  1. Find the missing angle. The angles of a triangle sum to $180^\circ$, so $$180^\circ-60^\circ-90^\circ=30^\circ.$$
  2. Assign variables. Let $a$ be the side adjacent to the $60^\circ$ angle and $b$ the opposite side.
  3. Use the $30^\circ$–$60^\circ$–$90^\circ$ ratio. The hypotenuse is twice the shortest side, so from $20=2x$ we get $x=10$, hence $a=10$, and $$b=\sqrt3\,x=3\cdot10=10\sqrt3.$$
Example 4

Find the measure of the missing angle and the lengths of the missing sides in the right triangle shown, where one angle is $30^\circ$ and the adjacent leg is $7$.

  1. Find the missing angle. $$180^\circ-90^\circ-30^\circ=60^\circ.$$
  2. Label the sides. Let $c$ be the hypotenuse and $b$ the side opposite the $30^\circ$ angle.
  3. Find $c$ with cosine. $$\cos30^\circ=\frac{7}{c}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c=\frac{7}{\cos30^\circ}=\frac{7}{\sqrt3/2}=\frac{14}{\sqrt3}$$
  4. Find $b$ with tangent. $$\tan30^\circ=\frac{b}{7}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad b=7\tan30^\circ=7\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt3}=\frac{7}{\sqrt3}$$

Note that we could have solved Example 4 using the method from Example 3, just as Example 3 could be solved with trigonometric ratio values.

6Applications

Right triangle trigonometry has many practical applications. Being able to compute the lengths of sides of a triangle makes it possible to find the height of a tall object without climbing to the top or extending a tape measure.

Two angles of view come up constantly:

The angle of inclination (also called the angle of elevation) is the angle whose initial side is a horizontal base line and whose terminal side is the line of sight to an object above the base line. The angle of depression is the angle from the horizontal base line to a terminal side below it — what you see if you start looking horizontally and then look down.

Example 5

The angle of inclination from a point on the ground $30$ feet away from the base of a water tower to the top of the tower is $60^\circ$. Find the height of the water tower to the nearest foot.

Let $h$ denote the height of the tower. Then

$$\tan60^\circ=\frac{h}{30}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h=30\tan60^\circ=30\sqrt3\approx51.96.$$

Hence, the water tower is approximately $\boxed{52\text{ feet}}$ tall.

Example 6

To determine the height of a California redwood tree, two sightings from the ground, one $200$ feet directly behind the other, are made. If the angles of inclination are $45^\circ$ and $30^\circ$, how tall is the tree to the nearest foot?

We have two unknowns: the height $h$ and the distance $x$ from the base of the tree to the first observation point. Using trigonometric ratios,

$$\tan45^\circ=\frac{h}{x},\qquad \tan30^\circ=\frac{h}{x+200}.$$

Since $\tan45^\circ=1$, the first equation gives $\frac{h}{x}=1$, so $x=h$. Substituting into the second equation,

$$\tan30^\circ=\frac{h}{h+200}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{1}{\sqrt3}=\frac{h}{h+200}$$

$$h\sqrt3=h+200\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h\sqrt3-h=200\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h(\sqrt3-1)=200$$

$$h=\frac{200}{\sqrt3-1}\approx273.205$$

Hence, the tree is approximately $\boxed{273\text{ feet}}$ tall.

Example 7

To what length must an adjustable ladder be set so that it reaches a windowsill $50$ feet above the ground, with the ladder resting against the building at an angle of $75^\circ$ with the ground?

The angle of inclination is $75^\circ$ and the opposite side is $50$ feet long. The hypotenuse $h$ is the length of the ladder we need. Using sine,

$$\sin75^\circ=\frac{50}{h}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h=\frac{50}{\sin75^\circ}$$

$$h\approx\frac{50}{0.9659258}\approx51.7638$$

Since $75^\circ$ is not a standard angle, a calculator gives us the approximate value of $\sin75^\circ$. The ladder should be set to approximately $\boxed{51.8\text{ feet}}$.

Using a calculator

In the real world it is rare to find angles of exactly $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$, or $60^\circ$. For other angles you will need a scientific or graphing calculator — an arithmetic calculator cannot do trigonometry.

  • Check your angle mode first. The calculator must be set to degrees rather than radians.
  • On a scientific calculator: punch in the angle measure, then the desired key — sin, cos, or tan.
  • On a graphing calculator: punch in the desired function first, then the angle, then enter or =.
  • For cosecant, secant, or cotangent: find sine, cosine, or tangent first, then apply the $x^{-1}$ key.
